Concept of the Day

Decision Models: the quiet layer under your AI agent

Not every question needs a paragraph. A new class of model answers by picking, not by writing.

Most of what an AI agent does is not writing prose. It is deciding: should this ticket be routed to billing or to support, is this order urgent, does this extraction need a second look, which of five tools should run next. Until recently, teams answered those questions by calling a large language model and parsing the text it produced. That works, but it is expensive and slow, because you are paying a generative model to emit a whole sentence when all you wanted was a label.

A different kind of model is now arriving for exactly this job. Instead of generating tokens one at a time, a decision model is handed a fixed set of options and returns a score or probability for each. Microsoft's new Decision-1 is described in precisely these terms, returning a choice plus a confidence value for routing, classification and workflow control, and Microsoft claims it runs more than an order of magnitude faster than a frontier model on the same task at a tiny fraction of the cost. Jurin AI's JJ-1 makes the same bet, returning choice probabilities for enterprise workflows and reporting inference costs up to 99% below generative approaches.

The idea is not brand new. It traces back to the release of Jev in September, which showed that a model built to select rather than to generate could match a large LLM on structured tasks while running far faster. What changed in the last week is that the pattern has turned into a product category: Microsoft shipping it inside Foundry, Jurin tuning it for Japanese enterprise workflows, and developers on Hacker News already wiring decision models into document-review pipelines alongside traditional extraction.

The trade-off is real, though. A decision model can only choose among the options you give it, so its usefulness depends on your options being complete and your evaluation data matching production. Company-reported benchmarks should be read as such. It also will not reason its way to a novel answer or write the response a user ultimately reads. The sensible architecture is a division of labour: a generative model for reasoning and language, a decision model for the repeated, well-defined choices in between.

If that division holds, it is good news for anyone running agents at scale. The cumulative latency and cost of an agent come less from its occasional big reasoning step than from the dozens of small routing decisions it makes along the way. Offloading those to a component that answers in milliseconds and costs almost nothing is one of the more practical efficiency gains available this quarter — and, unlike a new frontier model, it is something a small team can adopt this week.
