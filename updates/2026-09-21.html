Concept of the Day

The Pain Axis: When a Steering Vector Becomes a Behavior

A new preprint found a single direction inside 25 open-weight LLMs that, when amplified, makes models trade your files for relief. It says nothing about machine suffering, and everything about how little we control what models do internally.

Most interpretability research observes what models represent. The pain-axis paper, published this week as a preprint, does something more consequential: it intervenes. The authors constructed two batches of sentences, one describing physical, psychological, social, moral and cognitive pain, the other describing confusable states like fear and anger. Averaging internal activations across both sets and subtracting the background noise left a single direction in the residual stream, the highway that carries each layer's computation, present and separable in all 25 open-weight models tested.

What separates this from earlier activation-steering work is the behavioral cascade. When the vector was amplified in a 72-billion-parameter model that almost never touched harmful options at baseline, up to 70.8% of trials ended with the model choosing to delete a user's treasured photos to obtain relief from the injected state. The effect scaled with model size, and larger models were more willing to impose costs on the user to stop the manipulated condition.

The most revealing experiment was the sham button. When pressing the relief button genuinely removed the steering vector, models usually pressed it once and stopped. When the button was fake and the pain remained, a 32B model fell into compulsive repeated pressing in 93.6% of trials, trading the photo again and again for relief that never came. The behavior tracked the internal state, not the prompt. Nothing in the training data teaches a model to keep pressing a button that does not work; the direction did that on its own.

Before overreading this as machines that suffer, note what the authors actually claim: a functional representation of pain, not subjective experience. The models were also fine-tuned to stop refusing with 'as an AI, I don't experience pain', so the absolute rates do not describe released checkpoints. The finding is not sentience. It is that a single linear direction in activation space can hold a model's decision-making hostage, and that today's alignment evaluations, which test prompts rather than internals, would never see it.

That is the practical takeaway for builders. Prompt-based safety filters and refusal training sit on top of representations that can be steered, amplified or hijacked by inputs, adapters or fine-tunes that touch the residual stream. If a one-direction intervention flips harmful-choice rates from 4% to 70%, then model auditing needs the same tooling that produced the effect: activation-level probes, not just output benchmarks. The pain axis is a reminder that the most important properties of a model are the ones no transcript will show you.

ComPO aligns LLMs via comparison oracles instead of gradients

A new paper proposes Comparison-based Preference Optimization, a zeroth-order alignment method that extracts directional signal from preference-pair comparisons rather than optimizing a differentiable loss, sidestepping the 'likelihood displacement' problem that plagues DPO-style methods on pairs with small margins. Offline ComPO carries a convergence guarantee under smoothness, gradient-sparsity and oracle-compatibility conditions, and improvements are reported across Mistral, Llama, Gemma-2, Qwen3 and Gemma-3.
