Concept of the Day

The Price of a Thought

Why the token price is collapsing, and what it does to the agent economy

For three years the question about a new model was how smart it was. This week the question changed. Anthropic launched Fable 5.2 with benchmark gains in the low double digits and led its announcement with a price cut, from $10 to $8 per million input tokens. Meta launched Muse Ember and led with ten cents. Google cut Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash video by 30%. Three labs, one day, and the headline number each chose was money.

The reason is that the buyer changed. When a person chats with a model, capability dominates and price is noise. When an agent works through a task, the model is called dozens or hundreds of times, and every call that is ten percent cheaper compounds across the whole trajectory. Cost per completed task, not cost per token, is the number that decides whether an agent deployment survives its first budget review.

That is why Meta's framing of Ember is instructive. Ember is not trying to be the smartest model; Meta positions it as the volume layer beneath a larger planner, the worker that handles tool calls, routing and extraction while the expensive model thinks. The industry is quietly adopting a two-tier architecture: a small, fast, nearly free model for execution and a large model for planning. Once that split exists, the volume tier is where the token count actually lives, and the price war moves there.

Research is attacking the same problem from the other end. CPU-ARC takes a reasoning trace that a frontier model produced once and compiles it into a CPU-executable artefact that handles new instances of the same task at 16 to 291 times lower invocation cost, with no model in the loop. Energy-Based Reasoners give a looped model a principled signal for when to stop thinking, reaching 99.9% on Sudoku-Extreme with 345 times less inference compute than the next-best model. Both papers are saying the same thing: most of what we spend on inference is spent re-deriving things we already know.

The consequence is not that models get worse. It is that the unit of competition becomes the solved task, and the interesting engineering moves up a layer: caching, routing, stopping rules, and knowing which questions are worth a large model at all. The labs that win the next year may not be the ones with the best benchmark row.

There is a counterweight worth naming. Anthropic's filing shows about $895 billion in compute and infrastructure commitments against $33 billion of nine-month revenue, and a separate $3.4 billion for gas generators to power it. Cheap tokens are being sold on top of extremely expensive capacity. If price per token keeps falling while commitments stay fixed, the margin has to come from volume, and volume has to come from agents doing work that someone pays for.

Research

Energy-Based Reasoners give looped models an explicit stopping signal

Looped models refine a latent state by repeating a recurrent block but have no explicit measure of progress. Energy-Based Reasoners learn an energy function over latent reasoning states and treat each loop as gradient descent on that energy, so the same function scores states and supplies a stopping criterion. Across four algorithmic reasoning benchmarks the authors report parity or better against prior recurrent reasoners, 99.9% accuracy on Sudoku-Extreme with 345 times less inference compute than the next-best model, and state-of-the-art coverage of distinct valid solutions on multi-solution tasks.

Research

Forge Decision Context lifts agent rule compliance to 95% with ten times fewer tokens

The paper notes that agents do not learn from corrections across sessions, so users fix the same mistake every time, and that existing memory systems store facts rather than procedural knowledge. Forge Decision Context captures behavioural signals from human corrections and self-assessed tool-call failures, compiles them into imperative rule directives, and injects each rule at the decision point where it applies. On a new 71-scenario benchmark built from real agent traces it reports 95% compliance versus 55% for naive retrieval while injecting about ten times fewer tokens per query.

Safety

EMERGE benchmark separates eight kinds of emergent misalignment that one score hides

Emergent misalignment, where fine-tuning on a narrow task causes misaligned behaviour on unrelated prompts, is usually measured with a small question set whose rates depend on the judge and thresholds. EMERGE tracks eight distinct misaligned behaviours from base model to fine-tuned model on out-of-domain prompts, compares them against matched benign controls, and monitors capability, coherence and refusal as confounders. Across eight open-weight models and 13 fine-tuning datasets, models with similar overall misalignment rates showed very different behavioural profiles.
