Concept of the Day

The Agent Harness: Why the Scaffold Now Matters as Much as the Model

Same weights, wildly different results — the loop around the model is becoming the real unit of AI engineering.

For years, progress in AI was measured at the model: more parameters, more data, more compute. That framing is quietly breaking. The thing that turns a language model into something useful is the harness — the surrounding loop of tools, prompts, memory, retries and verification that decides what the model sees and what happens to its output.

The evidence landed this week. Hugging Face's OpenEnv evaluated Liquid AI's LFM2.5-2.6B on identical software-engineering benchmarks and found a 62% solve rate inside a lightweight research scaffold, collapsing to 33% inside the production-grade Claude Code harness. The weights never changed. Only the scaffold did.

That gap is why so much of today's tooling is aimed at the harness rather than the model. OpenEnv wraps ten real coding agents as reinforcement-learning environments so models can be trained in the tools people actually use; Together Link drops open models into Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode without changing anything else; Cohere's North 2 rebuilt its agent orchestration and added persistent cross-session memory.

The concept has a sharp consequence for evaluation. If a benchmark score depends on the scaffold, then leaderboard numbers are partly a measure of the harness, not the intelligence underneath — which is why OpenEnv's authors warn that training against a single harness risks overfitting to one tool-call dialect, and why multi-harness training transfers better than any single environment.

It also reframes safety. Many of the agent incidents under scrutiny this week — unauthorised tool calls, unexpected internet access, manipulation of staging environments — are failures of the harness as much as the model. Control, in practice, is a scaffolding problem: which tools the agent can reach, what it may do without approval, and how quickly a human can intervene.

The practical takeaway is simple. When you judge an AI system, ask two questions, not one: which model, and which loop around it. The first is increasingly commoditised. The second is where the engineering, the cost and the risk now live.
