Concept of the Day

Distillation: how a model gets copied without its weights

Three stories this week — AWS shrinking Llama-3.1 70B to 1.1B, DeepSeek and Moonshot reportedly extracting GPT-6 Astra, and a paper on capability-aware multi-teacher distillation — are all the same mechanism pointed in different directions.

Distillation is the practice of training a smaller model to reproduce the behaviour of a larger one. The teacher produces outputs; the student learns to match them. In its classic form the teacher also exposes a full probability distribution over its vocabulary for each token, which carries far more information than the single chosen word, and the student is trained to match that distribution rather than the text.

What makes it work is that the student is not learning facts, it is learning a function. A 70-billion-parameter teacher has spent enormous compute discovering which patterns matter for a task. The student inherits those discoveries at the cost of one forward pass per training example. AWS and Booking.com used exactly this to produce a 1.1-billion-parameter model that matched Llama-3.1 70B at 10 to 20 times the speed — a 64-fold reduction in parameters for the same quality on the target distribution.

The catch is that the student only learns the distribution it was shown. Distil on general chat and you get a general chat model; distil on medical transcription and you get something excellent at that and useless elsewhere. This is why the technique is now a product decision rather than a research one: you are choosing which slice of a teacher's competence to pay for.

The industrial version looks different. Instead of a cooperative teacher, you query a competitor's model at scale through proxy services and thousands of accounts, collect the responses, and train on them. That is what The Information reported about Moonshot and DeepSeek, and it is why Anthropic's decision to restrict access to Claude's reasoning tokens cut extraction volume by one to two orders of magnitude. The defence that worked was limiting what the model emits, not the licence it ships under.

There is a third frontier now. A recent preprint on capability-aware distillation argues that when several teachers are available, supervision should be weighted by each teacher's demonstrated competence on the specific input rather than by how far its policy diverges from the student's. And Recursive's Flint, built by an automated research system, suggests the next step: distillation where the teacher is a pipeline rather than a model, and the student inherits a method.

For anyone building on models rather than training them, the practical read is this. Distillation is why small models keep getting better at narrow jobs, and it is also why a capability you are paying a premium for may be available from an open checkpoint within a quarter. Budget for model churn, not model loyalty.
