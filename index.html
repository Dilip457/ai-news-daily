Concept of the Day

The Verification Turn

Why checked claims are replacing impressive ones

Something changed in how AI results are being announced this week. A startup published a proof of a conjecture open since 2008 and pointed at Lean, the proof assistant, as the witness. Alibaba published a coding benchmark built from real user sessions that had already defeated agents, and reported that the best model fully solves 26.4% of them. METR published an eleven-week audit of a lab's internal research velocity. In each case the claim arrives with a mechanism that can say no.

That is a different posture from the benchmark table. Vendor tables are chosen by the vendor, on tasks the vendor found tractable, and they saturate: the same models that score above 99% on older coding benchmarks finish roughly one task in four on Co-Design Bench. A number that cannot fail is not a measurement, it is marketing with decimals.

Verification has a cost, and the cost is where the interesting failures live. METR's audit found Anthropic's capability growth running at about 1.9 times its earlier rate, with roughly 80% attributed to internal AI automating research, and then much of the supporting evidence was redacted before publication. A Lean proof can be checked line by line, but someone still has to confirm the formal statement matches the informal problem. GPT-6 Cyber's 948 kernel vulnerabilities are real patches, but the same model finds exploits.

So the honest reading is narrower than the headlines. Machine-checkable results are the strongest evidence we have that AI is contributing to hard technical work, and they are also the easiest to over-read. A proof of one conjecture is not a method for proving conjectures. A benchmark showing 26.4% is not a forecast of next year.

What to watch is whether verification becomes routine or stays ceremonial. If pre-release audits by independent evaluators, as the three big labs now say they are coordinating on, produce published evidence rather than joint press releases, the gap between claim and check narrows. If they produce statements of intent, we will keep getting the same trade: louder numbers, thinner proof.

The State of AI 2026-11-09

The week's most consequential story is not a model release. Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang's relationship with a Megaspeed executive whom US officials reportedly assessed as working for Chinese intelligence, with a committee of independent directors and outside counsel overseeing the review. The board says it has no reason to believe secrets were compromised; Huang has said he will cooperate. Three House committee chairs have asked him to brief Congress on 18 November, a day after earnings.

Money moved in the opposite direction from capability. OpenAI closed about $11 billion of a round valuing it near $1.25 trillion, well short of the $30 billion it sought, and Anthropic's expected IPO valuation has slid from around $2 trillion toward $1 trillion to $1.5 trillion ahead of a mid-November Nasdaq debut, with its amended filing disclosing about $895 billion in compute and infrastructure commitments. Anthropic also agreed to pay $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts of gas-fired generators, the largest known single power-equipment purchase by an AI lab.

On the model side the open-weight frontier got crowded in a few days. Mistral published full Mistral Large 4 weights: a 1T-parameter multimodal mixture-of-experts with 1M context, 41% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 and a modified-MIT licence that requires a commercial agreement for model-as-a-service resale. Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Small, a 594B MoE pretrained in 68 hours in NVFP4 with Apache 2.0 weights promised after safety testing. Anthropic shipped Claude Sonnet 5.5 at 70.6% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, ahead of Opus 5.5.

Safety research produced the week's sharpest numbers. METR's audit put Anthropic's capability growth at about 1.9 times its prior rate, roughly 80% from internal AI automating research, with substantial evidence redacted. Physical Superintelligence reported a Lean-checked proof of the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture, open since 2008, from a non-public Anthropic model, not yet peer reviewed. DeepMind said AI agents found two ways to weaken legacy 1024-bit RSA, and OpenAI's GPT-6 Cyber, its first model rated Critical for cyber capability, swept 948 Linux kernel flaws in one run.

For builders, the practical signal is in the benchmarks that refused to flatter. Co-Design Bench, drawn from 37,463 real coding queries, found the best agent fully solves 26.4% of tasks while the same models clear 99% on older suites. MemHarmBench found 59% to 76% attack success for cross-session prompt injection, with most successful attacks leaving no trajectory anomaly. Budget for the gap, not the leaderboard.
