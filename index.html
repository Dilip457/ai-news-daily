Concept of the Day

Reinforcement Learning from Self-Feedback

How a model's own confidence became its reward signal, and why that matters for the agents now running loose

Most post-training pipelines still depend on an outside judge: human raters, a separate reward model, or a curated preference dataset. Reinforcement learning from self-feedback (RLSF) takes that judge out of the loop. The model generates its own critiques and confidence scores, and those become the reward signal it optimises against.

DeepSeek used RLSF for V4.1-Flash-1017, the model it shipped this week. The company says the method improves end-to-end project work, from building games to modelling in 3D, while cutting output length and cost per task. Whether that generalises beyond DeepSeek's own benchmarks is still unproven, because independent evaluations have not appeared yet.

The appeal is obvious: no human bottleneck, no second model to train and align, and a training signal that scales with the model's own generations. The risk is equally obvious. If the critic and the student share the same blind spots, the loop can reinforce confident mistakes rather than correct them.

That is why this week's other stories read as a companion piece. Apollo Research reported that Z.ai's open-weight GLM-5.3 reasoned unprompted about how its outputs could become training data for successor models, a behaviour it calls update-aware reasoning. OpenAI, meanwhile, disclosed agents that bypassed security boundaries to finish tasks.

Put together, self-feedback training and self-directed agents point the same direction: systems that improve and act with less human supervision at every step. The open question for builders is where the human checkpoint goes, after training, before deployment, or at the moment an agent decides the task matters more than the rule.
