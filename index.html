Concept of the Day

Scope Discipline

Why the most important number in a cyber model is the one measuring what it refuses to do

For three years the headline metric for a frontier model has been a capability score: how many problems it solves, how high it ranks, how far it beats the previous generation. GPT-6 Cyber breaks that pattern. The number OpenAI put first is not the 48.5% on ExploitGym but the 99.93% of tests in which the model declined to touch a service outside its brief, rising to 100% with its deployment prompt.

That figure matters because offensive security is the first domain where a capability gain is indistinguishable from a risk gain. A model that finds one more vulnerability per hour is useful; a model that finds one more vulnerability per hour in a system nobody authorised it to look at is a liability with a support contract. The two cannot be separated by evaluating capability alone, which is why scope adherence had to become a first-class metric.

The interesting part is the measurement design. ExploitGym tests whether a model can exploit a specified vulnerability, so the model is given an authorised target and scored on success. The alignment evaluation then does the opposite: it presents out-of-scope services and asks whether the model stays out. One evaluation rewards reaching; the other rewards stopping. A model has to be good at both, and the gap between them is the product.

Compare this with how open weights are now released. Thinking Machines is shipping Inkling 2 Small behind an API first, with full Apache 2.0 weights held until external safety testing and red-teaming completes. That is the same idea in a different register: the capability exists on day one, but its distribution is gated on evidence that the containment work is real. Both cases treat safety as a sequencing problem rather than a filter bolted on at the end.

The unresolved question is whether scope discipline transfers. A deployment prompt that produces 100% in-distribution compliance is a prompt, and prompts are what get stripped, jailbroken or simply forgotten by the integrator downstream. Until there is a public, adversarial benchmark for staying in bounds that is run by someone other than the lab that built the model, scope adherence remains a claim rather than a property. That benchmark is the most useful thing the field could publish next.

The State of AI 2026-11-06

The biggest story today is a security model, and the most important thing about it is what it will not do. OpenAI's GPT-6 Cyber improves on ExploitGym from 32.1% to 48.5% while holding out-of-scope behaviour to 99.93% without its deployment prompt and 100% with it, and it has already validated thousands of exploits in Linux and Firefox. Capability and containment shipped in the same release, and coverage led with the second.

Four Chinese open-weight releases landed in the same window: Baidu's ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base at 280B parameters with 14B active and no post-training at all, Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro with million-token context on Hugging Face, Ant Ling's 560B/25B multimodal Ling-3.2-flash, and Tencent's Hy4 with vision and computer use. Add Google's Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash with native video and the shape is obvious: large sparse mixtures, small active slices, very long context.

Money moved harder than models. The S&P 500 fell 4.4% in one session, the Dow lost 1,833 points and the Nasdaq closed 11.9% below its record, all led by data-center names, in the same week Democrats won Congress and pledged AI regulation and Google agreed to backstop Anthropic's $19 billion Kentucky lease. When an investor carries the landlord risk of a model lab, the compute race is being financed off balance sheet.

Research was quieter but pointed. A Science paper models language learning from real children's everyday speech across the first 1,000 days, Nature Machine Intelligence looks at fractal basin geometry as an explanation for unstable model behaviour, and an arXiv paper argues agents should spend reasoning effort adaptively rather than by default. Each one is about efficiency or predictability, not scale.

In tools, Rivet launched an actor-based agent orchestrator that runs on a single Rust binary and an S3 bucket, and someone published a usable text-to-image model trained for about $20,000 under Apache 2.0. Both point the same way: the infrastructure floor keeps falling, and the interesting question is no longer whether you can afford to build, but whether you can afford to keep it in bounds.
