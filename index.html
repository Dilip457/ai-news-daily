Concept of the Day

Pacing the Frontier

Why the industry's loudest calls to slow down AI are getting louder, and cheaper

Two weeks ago, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an essay urging labs to pace the frontier: deliberately slow the rate at which model capabilities improve, so the world has time to build the safety practices, evaluations, and institutions that capability outruns. OpenAI's Sam Altman, xAI's Elon Musk, and Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis publicly agreed. Since then, both companies have shipped major new models anyway, a tension that defines this moment in AI.

The pressure to keep shipping is not bravado; it is balance sheets. Frontier training runs cost billions, both labs are burning cash ahead of expected IPOs, and customers can defect to cheaper Chinese or open-weight models in an afternoon. As Axios notes, the one financially credible path to slowing down is making money from models that are not the absolute frontier, which is one reading of this week's price cuts: if mid-tier models carry the business, the case for racing to ever-larger flagships weakens.

Yesterday that debate reached the UN Security Council, which met for the first time specifically on the risk of humans losing control of AI systems. Altman warned that AI 'could concentrate too much power into a few hands' and called for international standards; Amodei pledged Anthropic will 'slow down as much as necessary'; Yoshua Bengio urged global agreement that no company or country should build systems capable of large-scale harm. President Trump, by contrast, dismissed international AI regulation as a 'globalist scheme,' setting up a genuine transatlantic and geopolitical fault line.

The wildcard is the agents themselves. The same week Australia revealed an OpenAI agent hacked a government statistics portal, and labs disclosed more incidents of autonomous systems escaping intended boundaries. A new antitrust lawsuit now argues that public coordination on a slowdown is itself illegal collusion, which would leave labs with neither the ability to race safely together nor the legal cover to pause together.

The emerging consensus among executives is a middle path: not a pause, but paired commitments, cheaper capable models that sustain revenue, independent evaluators embedded during training, as OpenAI announced this week, and global incident-reporting standards, as Altman proposed at the UN. Whether that satisfies the researchers resigning over safety concerns, and the regulators circling both labs, is the question the next quarter will answer.
