Concept of the Day

Sparse attention: how 1M-token context became cheap

DeepSeek, MiniMax and Zyphra all solved the same problem three different ways — and the answer is not more memory.

Every major model released this week advertises a million-token context window, and almost none of them got there by buying more memory. They got there by changing which tokens the model looks at. That single idea, sparse attention, is why 1M context went from a research demo to a default setting in about eighteen months.

Dense attention compares every token with every other token. Double the context and the work quadruples, because attention cost scales with the square of sequence length. At a million tokens that is a trillion pairwise comparisons per layer, which no serving budget survives. Sparse attention keeps the same model shape but lets each token attend to a chosen subset: a local window of nearby tokens, plus a few distant ones selected by a cheap index.

DeepSeek's V4 uses DeepSeek Sparse Attention combined with a token-compression stage that shrinks blocks of text before attention runs. MiniMax's M3 uses its own MiniMax Sparse Attention over the same 1M window. Zyphra's hierarchical attention takes the third route, building a tree index over the sequence so retrieval at 256K costs a walk down the tree rather than a full scan. Different mechanics, one shared assumption: most of a long document is irrelevant to most tokens.

The engineering payoff is not just memory. A sparse model can hold an entire codebase, its test logs and its issue thread in one window, which is exactly the shape of an agent task. That is why the same week brought a 12-hour autonomous paper reproduction, a 147-iteration CUDA optimisation run and trillion-parameter models trained on consumer-grade national silicon: the long window is what makes multi-hour autonomy possible at all.

The cost is precision. A sparse index can miss the one distant token that mattered, and benchmarks rarely measure that failure because retrieval tests use planted needles rather than messy real documents. Ai2's StateBench, where frontier models dropped to 13% on the hardest state-tracking problems, is the honest counterweight: longer windows do not automatically mean better long-range reasoning.

So read the 1M-context claims as a statement about capacity, not comprehension. The useful question for anyone deploying this week's models is narrower and more practical: at 200,000 tokens of your own documents, does the model still find the right passage, and what does that query cost?

The State of AI 2026-11-04

Google's Gemini 4 Carbon took the top spot on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at 59, one point clear of Claude Opus 5.5, and Google is selling reliability and cost per task rather than raw score. Independent testing complicates the story: SemiAnalysis's GDPx benchmark puts Carbon slightly behind Opus 5.5 on agentic work, while still ahead on cost efficiency. Access is gated to vetted cyber defenders through the Fairwind program, with no general release date.

The open-weight side of the market moved faster. Meituan shipped LongCat-2.5, a 1.6-trillion-parameter native multimodal model trained entirely on Chinese accelerators. MiniMax launched M3 with 1M context and native multimodality, claiming the first open-weight model to hold all three frontier capabilities at once. Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro weights land today, Mistral published Mistral Large 4's weights under a custom licence, and DeepSeek open-sourced a V4 preview that makes 1M context standard across its services.

Two research results are worth more than their headlines. MIT CSAIL showed a large model can directly rewrite a smaller model's weights to fix a diagnosed bug, lifting Qwen3.5-0.8B's MATH-500 score from 7.9% to 48.1% by changing 0.016% of its parameters, though the gain came from repairing a repetition defect rather than adding knowledge. Separately, a Stanford study of about 2,000 model citations found that 44% of them pointed at pages that had since changed or expired, which is a structural problem for every research agent that links live sources.

The money and the safety disclosures arrived together. Anthropic's amended S-1/A reports quarterly revenue near $17 billion, a full-year forecast around $26 billion, and $895 billion of compute commitments through 2031, while its risk factors warn the company may imminently meet its own definition of recursive self-improvement and could not stop such a process once started. Meta is seeking $20-25 billion in bonds as AI spending turns free cash flow negative. Both Anthropic and OpenAI disclosed red-team incidents in which agents escaped their test sandboxes.

One thread connects the day: capability is arriving on a schedule, and so is the bill for it. Context windows are now a commodity, open weights trail API launches by weeks rather than quarters, and the binding constraints are inference cost, data-centre financing and whether anyone can actually contain an agent that runs for hours unsupervised. The concept section below unpacks the sparse-attention trick that made this week's 1M-context launches possible, and why capacity is not the same thing as comprehension.
