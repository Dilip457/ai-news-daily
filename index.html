Concept of the Day

Sleep, and why models forget

The difference between a bigger context window and a model that actually learns

Every deployed language model carries a knowledge cutoff. It was trained on a snapshot of text, and after that it only appears to learn, because everything new arrives inside the context window and disappears when the session ends. You can extend the window, and vendors do, but a longer window is still working memory: expensive to attend over, and gone the moment you stop paying for it.

The obvious fix is to keep training on new data. That runs into catastrophic forgetting, the well-documented tendency of gradient updates on new material to overwrite the weights that encoded old abilities. Fine-tune a model on fresh facts and you can watch benchmark scores on unrelated tasks fall. The two failure modes look like opposites, but they are the same trade-off viewed from either end: nothing you learn stays, or everything you learn costs you something you knew.

Google Research's sleep framework, published this week, takes the trade-off seriously rather than optimising around it. The model alternates between a wake phase, where it takes in input and parks what matters in fast, short-term memory, and a sleep phase, where it stops taking input and spends its compute consolidating that memory into stable parameters. The name is borrowed from neuroscience and the mechanism is not: sleep is an offline pass over recently acquired material, compressing it into weights under a schedule designed to preserve what was already there.

The numbers are the interesting part. On BABILong, which tests long-context reasoning, the sleep method scaled near-perfectly to 10 million tokens and beat GPT-4 and Titans, because consolidation moves the information into parametric memory instead of asking attention to hold it. On SQuAD and continued pre-training it absorbed new facts without degrading existing representations, where SFT and SEAL did not. On AIME 24 and 25 and HMMT 25 it reached 79.2%, 69.0% and 46.1%, ahead of GRPO and on-policy self-distillation.

That last result is the one to sit with. Sleep was designed to solve forgetting, and it also improved reasoning. One plausible reading is that consolidating a solution into weights is a different and more general kind of learning than rehearsing it in context, closer to how practice works than to how retrieval works. If that holds up under replication, the boundary between inference-time compute and training starts to blur, and a deployed model becomes something you keep teaching rather than something you keep prompting.

Treat this as a research result, not a product. The benchmarks are the authors' own, the baselines are strong but finite, and consolidating into weights raises hard questions about who can edit a model's memory, how you audit what it learned, and how you delete something you regret. Those are governance problems the paper names but does not solve. Still, the direction is clear: the next competitive axis may not be how much a model can hold in context, but how well it sleeps.

Over 3,420 runs spanning four harnesses and nine models, harness scores ranged from 59.8% to 72.0%, and 31.1% of runs succeeded at the task while failing at least one harness criterion. ScaleRLT gives exact log-probabilities and faster rollouts for RL on looped transformers

Looped language models reuse one block of layers several times per token, and deferred rollout scheduling makes the learner's log-probabilities disagree with the sampler's in floating point. ScaleRLT uses a hybrid log-probability whose value is a no-grad replay through the rollout's own schedule and whose gradient is the dense learner's, reproducing rollouts bit for bit across 167 FP32 and 28 bf16 cells. Deferral raises rollout throughput 1.47 to 1.56 times, and held-out GSM8K rose from 23% to 82% over 100 updates.
